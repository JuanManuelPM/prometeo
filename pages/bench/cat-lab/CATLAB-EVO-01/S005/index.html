01

WHISKERS
ARE MAPS

Cats use whiskers as precise touch sensors. Each whisker is rooted deeply enough to relay tiny changes in pressure, helping a cat judge openings and nearby surfaces without staring at them.

That sensitivity is useful in low light, when close-range vision is less informative. The whiskers do not measure an opening like a ruler; they feed the cat a stream of tactile clues.

Lab note: never trim whiskers for appearance. They are functional sensory equipment.

02

TAILS
SPEAK

A tail can broadcast arousal and intention. A relaxed upright tail often accompanies friendly approach, while a rapidly lashing tail can signal agitation or intense focus.

Context matters: ears, posture, distance and movement complete the message. Reading one body part in isolation is less useful than watching the whole cat and what is happening around it.

Lab note: body language is a pattern, not a single symbol.

03

SLEEP
IS WORK

Cats spend much of the day resting because their activity pattern is built around short, intense bursts. Sleep and quiet rest preserve energy for exploration, hunting-like play and social interaction.

Domestic cats often adapt their active periods to household routines, but they still tend to cycle between alert bursts and long recovery windows.

Lab note: a calm hideaway is part of an enriched environment, not wasted space.

04

PLAY
HAS A SEQUENCE

Good play often resembles a hunting sequence: notice, stalk, chase, pounce and capture. Toys that let the cat complete several of those steps can be more satisfying than movement with no possible catch.

Short sessions can be enough. Ending with a successful capture gives the sequence a clear finish and can help keep the interaction predictable.

Lab note: rotate simple toys instead of keeping every toy available all the time.
