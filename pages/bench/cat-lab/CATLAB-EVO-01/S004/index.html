01 · Signal

Ears / tail / eyes

Cats communicate before they vocalize. Ear angle, tail height, pupil size, whisker position, and body tension work together as a moving signal system. One cue alone is ambiguous; clusters are more useful.

A relaxed cat tends to distribute its weight loosely and move with low urgency. A cat that needs distance often becomes visually smaller or more rigid before escalating to hissing, swatting, or retreat.

Read combinations, not isolated gestures.

Context changes meaning. A raised tail during greeting differs from an upright, puffed tail during surprise. Watch what happened immediately before the signal and what the cat does next.

02 · Territory

Routes / height / scent

Domestic cats map space as a network of routes, hiding places, elevated lookouts, feeding zones, and scent-marked edges. Secure territory is less about owning a room than being able to move through it predictably.

Vertical space can increase usable territory without increasing floor area. A shelf, chair back, or windowsill may function as a safe observation point when the floor feels busy.

A good route includes an exit.

When resources are shared, distance matters. Separating food, water, litter, sleep, and play areas can reduce forced encounters and make a small home feel more controllable.

03 · Hunt

Stalk / pounce / reset

Play often rehearses pieces of predation: orient, stalk, chase, grab, bite, and disengage. Short intense sessions can be more satisfying than leaving one toy moving forever.

Variation keeps the sequence interesting. Change speed, direction, hiding points, and pauses. Let the cat catch the target sometimes; endless near-misses can turn stimulation into frustration.

Movement with pauses feels more alive.

Hands are poor prey substitutes. Objects create distance, protect skin, and make the start and end of play easier to understand.

04 · Rest

Heat / cover / routine

Rest is active regulation. Cats cycle through many short sleep periods and often choose locations that balance warmth, visibility, cover, and distance from disturbance.

Predictable routines reduce the number of surprises a cat must evaluate. Feeding, play, and quiet periods do not need military precision, but recognizable patterns can make the environment easier to read.

Safety is often a place with choices.

Some cats rest near people without wanting touch. Proximity and consent are separate signals; allowing both makes social contact clearer.
