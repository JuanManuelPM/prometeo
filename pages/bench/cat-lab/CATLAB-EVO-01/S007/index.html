01
Chapter one · signal

The tail is punctuation.

Cats communicate with whole-body geometry. A tail held upright often marks a relaxed greeting, while a fast lash can signal rising arousal. The useful clue is not one pose in isolation but the combination of tail, ears, eyes, distance, and motion.

Because the signals are layered, observation works better than assumption. A slow approach and a pause give the cat room to choose the next move.

Lab note: read clusters of signals, not a single body part.

For humans, the practical rule is simple: notice, wait, then respond. That rhythm reduces accidental pressure and makes interaction easier to predict.

02
Chapter two · sound

Purr is not one sentence.

Purring is strongly associated with comfort, but context still matters. Cats may purr during contact, rest, anticipation, or stressful moments. Sound alone cannot tell the whole story.

Listen together with posture and activity. A loose body resting near a familiar person is a different scene from a tense animal in an unfamiliar environment.

Lab note: the same sound can carry different meanings in different contexts.

The Cat Radio below does not imitate a real animal. It generates a quiet local two-oscillator texture in the browser so the page remains self-contained and works without streamed audio.

03
Chapter three · territory

Rooms become maps.

Domestic cats organize space through routes, resting points, lookout positions, scent, and repeated routines. Height can matter because an elevated place changes what the animal can see and how easily it can disengage.

A good environment is therefore more than floor area. It offers choices: hide, climb, observe, move, scratch, rest, and approach on the cat's own terms.

Lab note: choice is a feature of the environment, not a decorative extra.

Small changes can reshape the map. Moving a bed, clearing a shelf, or changing a feeding location can alter the routes a cat prefers.

04
Chapter four · play

Hunt in miniature.

Play often borrows the sequence of stalking, chasing, pouncing, catching, and pausing. Short sessions can be more useful than one long session if they stay responsive to the cat's interest.

Movement matters. A toy that changes speed, disappears briefly, or travels away from the cat usually creates a more readable target than one held motionless in front of the face.

Lab note: end with a catch or a calm transition instead of endless pursuit.

There is no universal perfect routine. The point of the lab is to watch what the individual cat actually chooses and adjust from evidence.
