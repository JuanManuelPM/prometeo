Chapter one · attention

The moving thing wins.

Small changes in speed, contrast, sound or direction can pull a cat's focus faster than a large object that stays still. Attention is easiest to observe when the environment changes one thing at a time.

Watch eyes, ears, whiskers and body angle together. A head turn can be curiosity; a whole-body shift shows the stimulus mattered enough to reorganize posture.

LAB NOTE · Observe before interpreting. Record what changed and what happened next.

2

Chapter two · sleep

Rest is active work.

Cats alternate light rest with deeper sleep across short bouts. A curled body or half-open eyes can still belong to a state that changes quickly when a sound appears.

Avoid waking the animal to test responsiveness. Note naturally occurring sounds or movements and whether ears, eyes, head or the whole body respond.

LAB NOTE · Non-invasive observation keeps the measurement from becoming the main event.

3

Chapter three · territory

A room is a map of options.

Height, cover, exits, warm surfaces, familiar scent and distance from people shape where a cat chooses to pause. Territory behaves like a collection of routes and safe stations.

When a favorite station changes, look for replacement behavior: a new perch, a longer route or a different resting distance. Substitutions reveal which features mattered.

LAB NOTE · Compare locations by function: lookout, retreat, transit, warmth, proximity or play.

4

Chapter four · play

Play rehearses a sequence.

Stalk, pause, chase, pounce, grab, release. Cat play borrows pieces of predatory behavior, but can reorder or repeat them. The useful measurement is often the transition between actions.

Short sessions make transitions easier to see. If the cat disengages, the session is finished; restarting immediately would mix two different motivational states.

LAB NOTE · End the trial when the animal ends it. Voluntary disengagement is data.
